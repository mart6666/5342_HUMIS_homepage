About Us

The history of women in Mexico is a profound narrative of resilience, transformation, and leadership that spans from the pre-Columbian era to the modern day. In Mesoamerican civilizations like the Maya and Aztecs, women held vital, dual roles as spiritual guardians, healers, and economic contributors, even as they navigated strictly defined gender structures. The Spanish conquest in 1519 radically disrupted these traditions, imposing patriarchal colonial laws and Catholic structures that restricted women's autonomy. Yet, figures like Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz emerged during this time, defying social limitations to become one of the greatest scholars and poets of the Spanish Golden Age, laying early intellectual foundations for women’s rights.

The 19th and early 20th centuries marked a major turning point as Mexican women actively shaped the nation's political identity. During the Mexican War of Independence, figures like Josefa Ortiz de Domínguez risked their lives to pass critical intelligence to insurgents. Decades later, during the Mexican Revolution (1910–1920), thousands of women known as soldaderas took up arms, served as camp followers, smuggled weapons, and worked as nurses and journalists. Despite their immense contributions to the birth of modern Mexico, women had to fight for decades more to gain formal political recognition, finally securing the right to vote in national elections in 1953.

In recent decades, the feminist movement in Mexico has achieved historic milestones while tackling deep-seated systemic challenges. The 21st century has seen a dramatic shift toward political parity, culminating in the historic election of Claudia Sheinbaum in 2024 as Mexico's first female president. Today, Mexican women continue to lead powerful grassroots movements, driving national conversations around gender equality, labor rights, and an end to gender-based violence. Their ongoing advocacy continues to reshape the legal, social, and cultural landscape of the country.
